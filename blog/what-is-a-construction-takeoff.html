What Is a Construction Takeoff? A Plain-English Guide for Contractors

By Max Measure Estimators · November 11, 2026

Short answer

A construction takeoff is a measured list of every material and quantity needed to build a project, taken directly from the drawings: counts, lengths, areas, volumes and weights. It is the foundation of a cost estimate. A takeoff tells you how much; the estimate adds labor, pricing and markup to tell you how much it costs.

If you bid construction work, every number you submit starts with a takeoff. Get the quantities wrong and the price is wrong, no matter how good your unit costs are. This guide explains what a takeoff is, what goes into one, how it differs from an estimate, and how contractors get them done — in plain English.

What is a construction takeoff?

A construction takeoff (also called a quantity takeoff, material takeoff or QTO) is the process of measuring and counting everything shown on a set of plans and specifications, and the list of quantities that results. The estimator "takes off" the quantities from the drawings — for example, cubic yards of concrete, linear feet of wall framing, square feet of drywall, or the number of light fixtures — so they can be priced.

A takeoff answers one question: what, and how much, does this project need? It does not, by itself, say what the project will cost.

What does a takeoff measure?

Every item on a drawing is measured in the unit it will be bought or installed in. Most takeoff items fall into five types of measurement:

Measurement typeTypical unitExamples
Counteach (EA)doors, windows, plumbing fixtures, outlets, light fixtures
Linearlinear feet (LF)wall framing, pipe, conduit, trim, curbs, footings
Areasquare feet (SF) or squaresdrywall, flooring, roofing, paint, slabs, sheathing
Volumecubic yards (CY)concrete, excavation, backfill, gravel base
Weightpounds or tonsrebar, structural steel, miscellaneous metals

A good takeoff also records where each quantity comes from — the sheet, the room or grid line, and the assumptions made — so someone else can check it.

Takeoff vs. estimate: what’s the difference?

The two terms are often used interchangeably, but they are different steps. The takeoff comes first; the estimate is built on top of it.

Quantity takeoffCost estimate
Question it answersHow much material is needed?How much will it cost to build?
Main outputQuantities by item and unitMaterial, labor, equipment and total price
Includes pricing?NoYes
Typical useOrdering material, checking sub quotes, the base for pricingBids, budgets, change orders, go/no-go decisions

In practice, an estimate = quantities × unit costs, plus labor, equipment, overhead and profit. That is why an error in the takeoff carries straight through to the bid price.

How is a takeoff done, step by step?

The exact workflow depends on the trade and the software, but most takeoffs follow the same steps:

  1. Review the full set. Read the drawings and the specifications, plus any addenda. Notes, schedules and details often change quantities shown on the plan views.
  2. Confirm the scope. Decide exactly which trades and items are included — a general contractor needs every division; a subcontractor needs only their scope.
  3. Check the scale. Verify the drawing scale on each sheet before measuring, especially on PDF sets that may have been resized.
  4. Measure and count. Work through the drawings systematically, sheet by sheet or area by area, so nothing is missed or counted twice.
  5. Organize the quantities. Group items by trade or by CSI division (more on that below) so they can be priced and compared with sub quotes.
  6. Add allowances. Apply waste and overlap factors where the material requires them (for example, lapped sheet goods or cut lumber), based on the item and the installer’s practice.
  7. Check the work. Compare totals against the drawings and against similar past projects, and mark up the plans to show what was measured.

What is the CSI MasterFormat, and why are takeoffs organized by it?

Commercial takeoffs and estimates in the United States are commonly organized using MasterFormat, a standard published by the Construction Specifications Institute (CSI). MasterFormat sorts construction work into numbered divisions — for example, Division 03 Concrete, Division 06 Wood, Plastics and Composites, Division 09 Finishes, and Division 26 Electrical.

Organizing a takeoff by division makes it easier to:

  • match quantities to the project specifications, which usually follow the same numbering;
  • send each trade the exact scope it needs to price;
  • compare subcontractor quotes line by line; and
  • spot missing scope before bid day.

Manual vs. digital takeoff

Takeoffs used to be done by hand with paper plans, a scale ruler and highlighters. Today most are done on screen with digital takeoff software, which measures directly on PDF or CAD drawings.

Manual takeoffDigital takeoff
ToolsPaper plans, scale, highlighters, calculatorSoftware such as Bluebeam or PlanSwift on PDF/CAD files
SpeedSlower, especially on large setsFaster; repeat items can be counted quickly
RevisionsRe-measure by handOverlay revised sheets and update quantities
Audit trailMarked-up paper setsColor-coded, marked-up digital plans

Digital tools don’t remove the need for judgment. The estimator still has to read the drawings correctly, understand how the work is built, and decide what belongs in scope.

What are the most common takeoff mistakes?

Most takeoff errors come from process, not math. Watch for:

  • Wrong scale on a resized or reduced PDF sheet.
  • Missed addenda or revised sheets issued after the original set.
  • Ignoring the specifications, where materials, thicknesses or finishes are defined.
  • Double counting items that appear on more than one sheet, such as plan views and enlarged plans.
  • Scope gaps between trades — work that each subcontractor assumes is someone else’s.
  • No allowance for waste where cutting, lapping or breakage is normal for the material.

Who needs a construction takeoff?

  • General contractors need full takeoffs across all divisions to build a complete bid and check subcontractor numbers.
  • Subcontractors need takeoffs for their own trade — drywall, electrical, plumbing, concrete and so on — to price their scope.
  • Builders and developers use takeoffs and early estimates to set budgets and decide whether a project makes sense.
  • Suppliers use takeoffs to quote and package material orders.

Cost estimating is a recognized profession; the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics notes that construction cost estimators read blueprints and technical documents to prepare estimates and identify the material and labor a project requires.

Should you do takeoffs in-house or outsource them?

Doing takeoffs in-house keeps everything under your roof, but it takes estimator time away from other bids — and that time is often the bottleneck when several bid dates land in the same week. Many contractors outsource some or all of their takeoffs so they can bid more work without adding staff.

When you outsource, look for a takeoff that is organized by CSI division, delivered with marked-up plans so you can check it, and returned before your bid deadline.

Frequently asked questions

Is a takeoff the same as an estimate?

No. A takeoff measures the quantities on the plans — counts, lengths, areas, volumes and weights. An estimate uses those quantities and adds material prices, labor, equipment, overhead and profit to arrive at a cost or bid price.

What does QTO stand for?

QTO stands for quantity takeoff. It is another name for a construction or material takeoff: the measured list of quantities taken from a project’s drawings and specifications.

What files are needed for a takeoff?

A complete set of drawings — usually PDF, but CAD or BIM files also work — plus the specifications and any addenda. The scope of work and the bid date help the estimator focus on the right trades and deadline.

How long does a construction takeoff take?

It depends on the size of the project and the number of trades. At Max Measure Estimators, most takeoffs and estimates are delivered in 24–48 hours, and the delivery date is confirmed in the quote before work begins.

Does a takeoff include labor costs?

A pure quantity takeoff does not include labor or pricing. Labor hours and rates are added in the estimate stage. Max Measure Estimators offers both takeoff-only and fully priced estimates, including union or prevailing wage pricing on request.
