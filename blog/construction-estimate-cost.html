How Much Does a Construction Takeoff or Estimate Cost?

By Max Measure Estimators · November 17, 2026

Short answer

The cost of an outsourced construction takeoff or estimate depends on the work involved, not a flat rate. The main drivers are project size and complexity, how many trades are in scope, drawing quality, the type of estimate, and pricing rules such as union or prevailing wage. The fastest way to know is to send your plans for a quote.

"How much will it cost?" is the first question most contractors ask before they hire an estimator. It is a fair question, and the honest answer is that it depends on how much work your plans need. This guide explains what drives the price of a takeoff or estimate, so you can understand a quote and get an accurate one quickly.

We do not publish a price list here, because a number without your plans would be a guess. Instead, this page walks through the factors an estimator looks at before quoting.

How much does a construction takeoff or estimate cost?

There is no single price. The cost of a takeoff or estimate is set by the time and skill it takes to measure and price your specific drawings. A small single-trade takeoff and a full multi-division commercial bid estimate are very different jobs, so they are quoted differently.

Most estimating firms, including ours, quote each project after reviewing the plans. The quote should tell you the price, what is included and the delivery date before any work starts. If someone gives you a firm price without seeing the drawings, ask what assumptions they made.

What factors drive the price of an estimate?

The price follows the effort. These are the factors that change the effort the most:

FactorWhy it changes the work
Project sizeMore floors, rooms, units or site area means more to measure and check.
ComplexityCustom details, curved walls, multiple building types or phases take longer than repetitive layouts.
Number of tradesA full general contractor estimate covers many divisions; a single-trade takeoff covers one scope.
Estimate typeA quick takeoff (quantities only) is less work than a priced bid estimate with material and labor.
Drawing qualityClear, scaled, complete sets are faster; missing sheets, poor scans or conflicting addenda add time.
SpecificationsLong spec books and alternates must be read and reflected in the quantities and pricing.
Labor rulesUnion or prevailing wage pricing needs the right wage rates for the project location.
Output formatExtra breakdowns, custom templates or marked-up plans for every sheet can add work.

Location matters in another way, too. It does not change the effort of measuring, but it changes the unit prices in the estimate, because material and labor costs differ across the country.

Quick takeoff vs. full bid estimate: which costs more?

A quick takeoff usually costs less than a full bid estimate, because it stops at quantities. A takeoff answers "how much material?"; a bid estimate also answers "how much will it cost to build?" by adding material prices, labor, equipment and, if you want, overhead and profit.

DeliverableWhat you getRelative effort
Quick takeoffMeasured quantities by item and unitLower
Preliminary estimateEarly budget from schematic or partial drawingsLow to medium
Bid estimateFull quantities and pricing, ready to submitHigher
Change order estimatePriced added, deleted or changed workDepends on the size of the change

If you are not sure which one you need, read our guide to what a construction takeoff is, or see the estimate types on our services page.

How does the number of trades change the cost?

More trades means more scope to measure, more items to price and more coordination between divisions. A drywall contractor who only needs drywall, framing and finishes quantities is buying a narrow scope. A general contractor who needs every division, from sitework to electrical, is buying a much larger job.

Commercial estimates in the United States are often organized by CSI MasterFormat divisions, such as Division 03 Concrete, Division 09 Finishes and Division 26 Electrical. Each division in scope adds work. When you request a quote, list the trades or divisions you need so the estimator does not price scope you do not want.

Does union or prevailing wage pricing cost more to estimate?

It can add some work, because the labor side must use the correct wage rates for the job. On many federally funded construction projects, the Davis-Bacon Act requires prevailing wages, and the applicable wage determinations are published on SAM.gov. The estimator has to find the right determination for the county and the trade classifications, then apply those rates.

Union jobs need local agreement rates instead. Tell the estimator up front if your project is union, prevailing wage or open shop, because it changes both the labor pricing and the time to prepare it.

Why are cheap estimates sometimes expensive?

A low-cost estimate that misses scope can cost far more than it saved. If a takeoff misses a floor, skips a detail or double-counts an area, the bid price is wrong. Bid too high and you lose the job; bid too low and you may win work that loses money.

When you compare quotes, compare what is included, not just the price:

  • Are quantities organized by trade or CSI division, so you can check them?
  • Do you get marked-up plans showing what was measured?
  • Are material and labor both priced, or only quantities?
  • Is pricing based on the project’s location?
  • Are small revisions after delivery included?
  • Is the delivery date confirmed in writing?

The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics describes cost estimators as the people who collect and analyze data to estimate the time, money, materials and labor needed for a project (BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook). That is skilled work, and a good quote reflects it.

How can I get a faster, more accurate quote?

Send a complete package and a clear scope. The estimator can only quote what they can see. The more complete your package, the more accurate the quote and the fewer surprises later.

  1. Plans in PDF (most common), CAD or BIM, with all sheets and the latest addenda.
  2. Specifications, if the project has them.
  3. Scope: the trades or CSI divisions you need, and anything excluded.
  4. Estimate type: quick takeoff, preliminary, bid or change order.
  5. Location: the project address or zip code, for pricing.
  6. Labor basis: union, prevailing wage or open shop.
  7. Deadline: your bid date, so the delivery date can be confirmed.

Frequently asked questions

How much does a construction estimate cost?

It depends on the project. Size, complexity, the number of trades, the type of estimate, drawing quality and labor rules all change the work involved. Send your plans and scope for a quote; we confirm the price before any work starts.

Is a takeoff cheaper than a full estimate?

Usually, yes. A takeoff stops at measured quantities, while a bid estimate also prices material, labor and equipment. The exact difference depends on your project, so both are quoted after reviewing the plans.

Why won’t you give a price without seeing my plans?

Because the price follows the work, and the work depends on the drawings. Two projects of similar size can need very different effort. Reviewing the plans first lets us give you a firm quote instead of a guess.

How long does an estimate take after I approve the quote?

Most takeoffs and estimates are delivered in 24–48 hours. Large or complex projects may take longer, and we confirm the delivery date in the quote. We do not offer rush or same-day service.

What should I send to get an accurate quote?

Send the full plan set in PDF, CAD or BIM, specifications if available, the trades you need, the project location, the labor basis (union, prevailing wage or open shop) and your bid date.

Do you offer packages for contractors who bid often?

Yes. Monthly and hourly packages are available for contractors with regular bid volume. Contact us with your typical workload and we will suggest an option.
